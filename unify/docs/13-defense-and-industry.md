# Sections 17 & 18 — Defence Preparation and Industry Perspective

## 17.1 How faculty attack a project like this

They probe four things, in roughly this order:

1. **Do you understand your own system?** (shapes, wiring, why each block exists)
2. **Do you know its limitations?** (this is the real test — students who claim perfection lose)
3. **Is the novelty real?** (what exactly did you do that the base papers did not)
4. **Did you measure, or did you assume?** (numbers, or hand-waving)

The single best preparation: **say the limitation before they find it.** A student who opens with "our sensor's bandwidth is 25× below the training data's, here is why that is acceptable and what it does and does not let us claim" is graded as a researcher. A student who is caught by that question is graded as a hobbyist.

## 17.2 The question bank

### A. Conceptual — predictive maintenance and RUL

**Q1. Define RUL.**
Time remaining until a system crosses its failure threshold: `RUL(t) = T_f − t`. It is properly a random variable; we predict its conditional expectation given sensor history. Units differ per dataset — cycles, seconds, minutes — which is why we predict a normalised fraction of life and denormalise per dataset.

**Q2. Why is RUL harder than fault detection?**
Six reasons: labels require destroying machines (we have 740 units total, 32 bearings); the label is unobservable at inference time; it is regression not classification; degradation is non-linear (flat then hockey-stick); error costs are asymmetric; and future RUL depends on unknown future operating conditions.

**Q3. Difference between diagnostics and prognostics?**
ISO 13374 / OSA-CBM layers: state detection (L3) and health assessment (L4) are diagnostics — what is wrong now. Prognostics (L5) is how long until failure. Our project spans L1–L7 with novelty at L5.

**Q4. Why not just use MTBF?**
MTBF is a fleet average. A specific unit's remaining life can differ from the fleet mean by an order of magnitude. Condition-based prognostics conditions on *this* machine's sensor history.

### B. Datasets

**Q5. Is C-MAPSS real data?**
No — it is generated by NASA's C-MAPSS Simulink simulator of a ~90,000 lb-thrust turbofan. That is why we pair it with FEMTO and XJTU-SY, which are real accelerated-life experiments on physical bearings.

**Q6. How is the C-MAPSS test set labelled?**
Test engine series are truncated before failure; `RUL_FD00X.txt` gives the true RUL at each engine's last recorded cycle. We predict on the final window only, matching the standard protocol.

**Q7. Why did you drop six sensors in FD001?**
Sensors 1, 5, 10, 16, 18 and 19 have zero variance in FD001 — they carry no information. We detect this with a variance filter rather than hard-coding, so FD002–FD004 are handled automatically.

**Q8. How did you split train and test?**
By unit, never by window. Adjacent windows overlap by 97 %, so random window splitting leaks near-identical samples across the split and produces fictitiously low RMSE. For the bearing datasets we use leave-one-bearing-out cross-validation and report mean ± std.

**Q9. Why not CWRU bearing data?**
CWRU and MFPT are fault-*classification* datasets — seeded faults at fixed severity, not run-to-failure. They have no RUL label. Using them would be a category error.

### C. Preprocessing

**Q10. What is RUL capping and why does it help?**
Raw RUL is linear from cycle 1, but a healthy engine at cycle 1 and cycle 40 look identical to the sensors. Asking for different outputs from identical inputs is unlearnable noise. Capping at 125 gives a piecewise-linear target: constant while healthy, linear once degradation is detectable. It roughly halves FD001 RMSE — from ~25–35 uncapped to 12–16 capped.

**Q11. Why z-score and not min-max?**
Min-max is set by extremes; one spike compresses everything else toward zero, and test data can exceed the training range. Z-score preserves outliers, which in prognostics *are* the signal.

**Q12. How do you handle three different time units?**
We predict normalised RUL — the fraction of capped life remaining, in [0,1] — so the loss is dimensionless and all three datasets contribute equally to the gradient. Physical units are recovered at inference by multiplying by the per-dataset scale stored in `norm_stats.json`. Metrics are always reported in physical units, per dataset, for comparability with literature.

**Q13. Why per-condition normalisation on FD002/FD004?**
Those subsets have six operating conditions whose effect on sensor readings exceeds the degradation signal. We cluster the three operational-setting columns with KMeans(6) and z-score within each condition. It is the highest-leverage preprocessing step on those subsets.

**Q14. What happens if you compute normalisation statistics at inference time?**
You normalise away the degradation trend itself. Predictions look stable and are meaningless — and it fails silently, with no exception. The training statistics are serialised and shipped with the model.

### D. Model

**Q15. Draw your architecture with shapes.**
`(B,S,L) → reshape (B·S,1,L) → shared 1-D CNN → (B·S,D) → reshape (B,S,D) → + sensor & domain embeddings → cross-attention with M learned queries and a padding mask → (B,M·D) → MLP → sigmoid → (B,1)`. Practise until it takes 90 seconds on a whiteboard.

**Q16. Why 1-D and not 2-D convolution?**
2-D convolution assumes both axes are translation-invariant. Along the sensor axis that is false — column order in the CSV is arbitrary, and neighbouring columns are not physically related. A 2-D kernel spanning `k` sensors also hard-codes the sensor count, which is exactly the problem we are solving. We convolve along time only and treat sensors as a set.

**Q17. Why is the encoder shared across sensors?**
Three reasons: sensor-count agnosticism (S only affects a reshape, never a weight shape); data efficiency (21 sensors give the encoder 21× more training examples); and cross-dataset transfer ("detect a monotone drift" is the same operation for turbine temperature and bearing RMS).

**Q18. But different sensors need different processing — isn't sharing wrong?**
The shared encoder learns universal shape primitives; the **sensor embedding** restores identity. Separation of concerns: shape extraction shared, identity learned per sensor. We also add a small domain embedding so the model can apply rig-specific corrections.

**Q19. Explain attention.**
`Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V`. Queries say what we're looking for, keys advertise what each sensor contains, values are the content. Dot products score every key against the query; softmax turns scores into weights summing to 1; the output is the weighted blend of values. The `√d_k` keeps the dot products at unit variance so softmax doesn't saturate and kill gradients.

**Q20. Why cross-attention and not concatenation?**
Concatenation produces a `(B, S·D)` vector, so the next linear layer's weight shape is welded to S. Cross-attention with M learned queries outputs `(B, M·D)` regardless of S, learns a data-dependent per-sensor weighting, handles missing sensors via the mask, and gives free per-prediction interpretability through the attention weights.

**Q21. Your attention weights explain the prediction, then?**
They indicate which sensors the model weighted, which is useful. But the literature ("Attention is not Explanation", and the rebuttal) shows attention weights can be perturbed without changing predictions, so they are not a rigorous causal explanation. We present them as an indication of feature usage.

**Q22. Why is the NASA score asymmetric?**
`exp(d/10)−1` for late predictions versus `exp(−d/13)−1` for early ones. A 50-cycle late prediction costs about 3× a 50-cycle early one, because predicting too much remaining life means the engine fails in service, while predicting too little merely wastes life. We train on SmoothL1 for stability and report the NASA score in physical units, optionally adding a clamped asymmetric term.

**Q23. Why not an LSTM?**
Sequential computation, so it maps poorly onto Cortex-A72 SIMD; 10–50× more parameters; and it is still sensor-count-fixed. Our model is 44k parameters and runs in 1–4 ms on the Pi. We report the comparison table.

**Q24. Why not a Transformer, like your base paper?**
DAST achieves ~11.4 RMSE on FD001 versus our 13–17, but it has a fixed input dimension — exactly problem P1 — and 1–10M parameters. It cannot run on XJTU without being rebuilt and retrained. We traded ~2 RMSE for sensor-count agnosticism, joint multi-domain training, and 2 ms edge inference.

**Q25. What about Graph Neural Networks — they handle variable node counts naturally?**
Correct, and they are the closest genuine competitor. But a GNN needs an adjacency matrix expressing sensor relationships, which we do not have and which differs per dataset. Learning the adjacency turns a GAT over a fully-connected graph into self-attention, so we use attention directly without the extra machinery.

**Q26. How many parameters and how large is the file?**
≈44k parameters, ≈176 KB fp32, ≈44 KB int8. Deliberately sized to stay inside the Pi's 1 MB shared L2 cache.

**Q27. Why does a network need non-linear activations?**
Without them a stack of linear layers collapses: `W₃(W₂(W₁x)) = (W₃W₂W₁)x = Wx`. Depth would add nothing.

### E. Deployment

**Q28. Difference between ONNX and ONNX Runtime?**
ONNX is the file format and operator specification — a document. ONNX Runtime is Microsoft's engine that loads and executes it with graph optimisations and hardware-specific execution providers. Format versus engine.

**Q29. What are dynamic axes and why do you need them?**
Tracing freezes every dimension at the dummy input's size. `dynamic_axes={"x":{0:"batch",1:"sensors"}}` marks batch and sensor count as symbolic. Without the sensor axis marked, the exported model is welded to 21 sensors and the entire "Any Dataset" claim collapses at export time. We verify with S ∈ {2,14,16,21}.

**Q30. Why not run PyTorch on the Pi?**
~800 MB installed versus ~50 MB for `onnxruntime`, slower import, painful ARM builds, and it locks us to one framework. ONNX is the portability contract in our project title.

**Q31. What is your inference latency?**
[Your measured p50/p95/p99] on Cortex-A72 with N threads, at a recorded temperature with `get_throttled` = 0x0. Inference is 1–4 ms out of a ~1000 ms end-to-end path — the 1-second feature window dominates, not the model.

**Q32. What is operator fusion?**
ORT merges `Conv + BatchNorm + ReLU` into one kernel. At inference BatchNorm is an affine transform that folds directly into the convolution's weights and bias, so it costs literally zero at runtime.

**Q33. Would this run on a different device?**
Yes — same `.onnx` file, different execution provider: TensorRT on Jetson, NNAPI on Android, CoreML on iOS, QNN on Qualcomm. That is the point of ONNX and the demonstration of "Any Hardware."

**Q34. Did quantisation hurt accuracy?**
[Have the table: fp32 vs int8, size, latency, RMSE per dataset.] If you did not quantise: "At 2 ms and 176 KB we had no need; we ran the study to quantify the option and found X."

### F. Hardware and the live rig

**Q35. Your sensor samples at 1 kHz but your training data is 25.6 kHz. How is that valid?**
It is a real limitation and we state it explicitly. The MPU-6050's 1 kHz ODR and ~260 Hz DLPF bandwidth cannot see the kHz-region resonances that bearing spall impulses excite. What it *can* see is shaft rotation, imbalance at 1×, misalignment at 2×, looseness, and overall vibration level. So the rig demonstrates the live pipeline end to end and detects the low-frequency faults we induce; validated RUL accuracy comes from the datasets. Closing the gap requires an ADXL1002-class sensor, which is our stated future scope.

**Q36. So the RUL number on your dashboard isn't real?**
It is the model's output on live features from a domain it was not trained on, and we label it as such. The validated claim on the live rig is *detection*: we recorded a healthy baseline, induced imbalance across three trials, and the health index dropped repeatably by X% with an alarm threshold set at 3σ below the healthy baseline.

**Q37. Why an ESP32 rather than reading I2C from the Pi?**
Physical separation (I2C is a ~30 cm bus, not field wiring), deterministic sampling (Linux user-space is preempted; a bare-metal ISR is not), architectural realism (sensor nodes at machines, one gateway per line), and it demonstrates edge preprocessing — 1000 samples become 8 features before transmission.

**Q38. What is the I2C address of the MPU-6050?**
0x68 with AD0 low, 0x69 with AD0 high.

**Q39. Why does the motor's noise break I2C, and what did you do?**
Brushed commutation injects supply spikes and radiates EMI; I2C is open-drain and relatively low-noise-margin. Fixes: 0.1 µF across the motor terminals and to the case, bulk capacitance on the 12 V rail, short twisted I2C leads away from motor wiring, common ground, and in firmware a `Wire` timeout plus a bus-recovery routine.

**Q40. Why separate power supplies?**
Motor inrush and Wi-Fi TX spikes would brown out the Pi if shared. Separate 5 V/3 A and 12 V/2 A rails with a common ground reference.

### G. Results and methodology

**Q41. Your RMSE is worse than the papers you cite. Why?**
Those are dataset specialists with hard-coded 14-sensor inputs. Ours is one weight set serving 2-, 14- and 16-sensor inputs across three domains, at 44k parameters and 2 ms on ARM. We report both the specialist and the universal numbers and are explicit about the trade-off.

**Q42. How do you know your model isn't just learning a time counter?**
It cannot — the input is normalised sensor values with no time index. We verified by shuffling the time axis within windows: performance collapses, confirming the model uses temporal structure.

**Q43. What is your baseline?**
A RandomForest on hand-crafted features. Our model beats it by X. Any deep model that cannot beat a random forest on tabular-ish features has not justified itself.

**Q44. How reproducible are your numbers?**
Fixed, logged seed; 3 seeds per configuration; mean ± std reported. Prognostics results are high-variance because of the small number of units, so single-run numbers are not trustworthy.

**Q45. What was your biggest technical challenge?**
[Pick one, be specific, include a number.] Good candidates: reconciling three time bases into one dimensionless target; masking in cross-attention so padded sensors do not contaminate the softmax; motor EMI hanging the I2C bus until we added recovery.

## 17.3 Common mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Why it costs you | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Claiming the live RUL is validated | The one claim you cannot defend | Use the honesty framing; separate rig from datasets |
| Listing OpenCV in the stack | You will be asked where you use it | Remove it |
| Random window train/test split | Leakage; your numbers are fiction | Split by unit |
| Reporting only the mean latency | Real-time work cares about tails | p50/p95/p99 |
| Not saving normalisation statistics | Silent, catastrophic wrongness on the Pi | Ship `norm_stats.json` |
| Forgetting `model.eval()` before export | BatchNorm in train mode, wrong forever | Assert it in the export script |
| Quoting a vendor downtime-cost figure as fact | Easy to challenge | Cite it as an industry estimate |
| Saying "our architecture is better" | Invites a comparison you lose | Say "different objective, here are both numbers" |
| Not knowing the other member's module | Examiners deliberately cross-examine | Swap explanations weekly |
| Demo-day live network dependency | Captive portals, 5 GHz hotspots | Pi as access point + replay fallback |
| Uncapped RUL | RMSE ~30, not comparable to literature | Cap at 125 |
| No baseline | "How do you know deep learning was needed?" | RandomForest baseline |

## 18.1 How industry actually builds this

### The vendors and what they would each contribute

**Arm** — the substrate everything else runs on. Cortex-A (your Pi), Cortex-M (microcontroller tier), **Ethos-U55/U65 NPUs** for embedded inference, **CMSIS-NN** (hand-optimised neural kernels for Cortex-M), **KleidiAI** (optimised micro-kernels now integrated into ONNX Runtime, ExecuTorch and llama.cpp), and **Arm NN / Arm Compute Library** as an ONNX Runtime execution provider. Arm would say: your model is 44k parameters; it belongs on a Cortex-M55 + Ethos-U55 at milliwatts, not on a 6 W Cortex-A72. They would be right, and that is a superb "future scope" line.

**NVIDIA** — **Jetson Orin Nano/AGX** at the edge, **TensorRT** for compilation and INT8/FP8 optimisation, **Triton Inference Server** for serving, **DeepStream** for sensor pipelines, **Fleet Command / NVIDIA AI Enterprise** for over-the-air fleet management, **Omniverse/Isaac Sim** for simulating machine degradation to generate synthetic training data. They would fuse vibration with high-rate video and thermal imaging.

**Qualcomm** — **Dragonwing IQ-series** industrial SoCs, the **Hexagon DSP/NPU** via **QNN / SNPE** and the AI Hub. Their pitch is inference at fractions of a watt with 5G connectivity built in — the battery-powered wireless sensor node your project cannot currently build.

**Siemens** — the operational-technology incumbent. **Senseye Predictive Maintenance** (acquired 2022) for fleet-scale asset health, **Insights Hub** (formerly MindSphere) as the industrial IoT cloud, **SIMATIC S7 / IOT2050** edge devices, and integration into the plant's existing **SCADA/CMMS** so an alert automatically opens a work order. Siemens' differentiator is not the model — it is that the prediction lands in the maintenance planner's queue with the right spare part reserved.

**Bosch** — **Rexroth ODiN** (Online Diagnostics Network) for hydraulic and drive systems, the **CISS** (Connected Industrial Sensor Solution) multi-sensor node, and **Bosch IoT Suite**. Bosch's advantage is that they make the machines *and* the sensors, so they have physics models plus decades of failure data.

**GE Vernova** — **SmartSignal** (anomaly detection on gas turbines using physics-based digital twins, deployed for 20+ years), **APM (Asset Performance Management)**, formerly under the **Predix** platform. GE's approach is hybrid: a first-principles thermodynamic model of the turbine, with ML explaining the residual between predicted and measured behaviour.

**Honeywell** — **Forge Asset Performance Management**, process-industry-focused (refineries, chemical plants), heavy on **APR (Asset Performance Reliability)** and integration with **Experion** DCS.

### The pattern across all of them

1. **Physics + ML, not ML alone.** Hybrid/physics-informed models. A pure black box is a hard sell to a plant engineer who has run that turbine for 20 years.
2. **Fleet learning.** They train on thousands of identical assets, not 100 simulated engines. That is their moat, and it is why your multi-dataset generalisation idea matters — it is the academic proxy for fleet data.
3. **Uncertainty is mandatory.** No serious commercial system emits a bare point estimate. Prediction intervals, confidence levels, or survival curves.
4. **Integration is the product.** The model is maybe 10 % of the value; the other 90 % is CMMS/ERP integration, work-order generation, spare-parts logistics, and technician workflow.
5. **MLOps at fleet scale.** Model versioning, staged rollout, drift monitoring, A/B comparison, remote update. NVIDIA Fleet Command, Azure IoT Edge, AWS IoT Greengrass, balena.
6. **Standards.** ISO 13374 (condition monitoring architecture), **OSA-CBM** (the 7-layer model), ISO 10816/20816 (vibration severity), MIMOSA (data interchange). Citing these makes your architecture section look professional.

| Security | Plain HTTP on a LAN | TLS, mutual auth, signed firmware, secure boot, IEC 62443 |
| Deployment | `scp` | Signed OTA with staged rollout and rollback |
| Monitoring | Dashboard | Drift detection, model performance tracking, alert-quality metrics |

| Validation | Held-out test units | Field trials over months, with measured avoided-downtime ROI |
| Team | 2 students, 12 weeks | 20–100 engineers, multi-year |

**What your project genuinely shares with industry:** the train-centrally / deploy-to-edge split, the ONNX portability contract, feature extraction at the sensor node, the OSA-CBM layering, edge inference for latency and bandwidth reasons, and the asymmetric-cost framing of prediction error. Those are real, and worth naming.

**What is genuinely novel-ish in yours:** sensor-count-agnostic architecture trained jointly across heterogeneous domains, deployed to CPU-only ARM. Most academic RUL work does one dataset and never deploys. Most industrial work is single-asset-class by construction. The intersection is thinly populated — say that, carefully, without overclaiming.

## 18.3 The three sentences that should end your presentation

> **Problem.** Existing RUL models are welded to one dataset, one sensor count and one hardware target; three of our base papers demonstrate exactly this limitation.
>
> **Contribution.** UNIFY-RUL uses a shared per-sensor 1-D CNN with cross-attention over a variable-size sensor set and a dimensionless normalised-life target, trained jointly on C-MAPSS, FEMTO and XJTU-SY, and exported to ONNX with dynamic sensor axes — one 44k-parameter model, three datasets, 2- to 21-sensor inputs, running at [X] ms on a CPU-only ARM edge device.
>
> **Honest limits.** We trade roughly 2 RMSE against dataset-specialised state of the art; our live sensor's bandwidth is 25× below the training data's, so the motor rig demonstrates the pipeline and low-frequency fault detection rather than validated bearing RUL. Both are quantified in the report, and both have a stated path forward.

## Summary

- Faculty probe understanding, limitations, novelty and measurement — in that order.
- Pre-empt every limitation; being caught is worse than the limitation itself.
- Learn the 45 questions above; the top ten are Q10, Q12, Q15, Q17, Q20, Q22, Q29, Q31, Q35, Q41.
- Industry does hybrid physics-ML, fleet data, mandatory uncertainty, and treats integration as the product.
- Your genuine overlap with industry: train-central/deploy-edge, ONNX portability, edge feature extraction, OSA-CBM layering.

## Key takeaways

1. "We traded 2 RMSE for portability and edge deployment, and here are both numbers" beats any accuracy claim you could make.
2. The sensor-bandwidth gap is your project's defining honest limitation. Lead with it.
3. Cite ISO 13374 / OSA-CBM — standards references make an architecture section look professional.
4. Have measured numbers for latency, memory, parameter count and RMSE. "About" is a losing word in a viva.

## Practical implementation tips

- Print the question bank. Both of you answer all 45 out loud, timed, twice.
- For every claim on a slide, know the file and line that produces it.
- Prepare three figures you can put on screen instantly: the architecture with shapes, the per-dataset results table, and the Pi latency histogram.
- Bring: the rig, a phone hotspot or the Pi in AP mode, an HDMI adapter, a spare ESP32, a spare SD card, and the demo video.

## Suggested improvements for this project

1. **Add uncertainty** (quantile regression). It is the one thing every industrial system has that yours lacks, it solves your own stated P4, and it costs ~15 lines.
2. **Benchmark on a second device** so "Any Hardware" is demonstrated rather than asserted.
3. **Add a physics-informed feature** (envelope-spectrum energy at BPFO/BPFI). Moves you toward the hybrid approach every industrial vendor uses, and it makes the project read as mechanical engineering plus ML rather than curve-fitting.
4. **Write the limitations section first.** It is the section that earns the most respect and the one students always write last, badly.
